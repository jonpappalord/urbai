Main outcomes

What URBAI revealed.

Five findings that connect algorithms, mobility, emissions, inequality, and the design of more responsible urban services.

01
Navigation

Navigation services concentrate urban traffic.

Across multiple cities and providers, higher adoption reduced route diversity and channelled traffic and emissions onto a smaller set of roads. The effect is systemic: individually sensible recommendations can collectively reduce network diversity.

↗
02
Emissions

The environmental benefit depends on adoption and traffic load.

At low traffic levels, navigation services can reduce CO₂. Under heavy demand, benefits diminish and may reverse beyond a city- and service-specific adoption threshold. There is no universally beneficial adoption rate.

↗
03
Human–AI feedback

Individual diversity can coexist with collective inequality.

In next-venue recommendation simulations, users explored more diverse venues while collective visits became more concentrated on a limited subset of places. Improving an individual metric does not guarantee an equitable system outcome.

↗
04
Urban change

Relocation rules shape segregation and gentrification.

Agent-based models showed how socioeconomic inequality, relocation strategies, and urban density affect neighbourhood transitions. A temporal network measure detected gentrification earlier than conventional count-based indicators.

↗
05
Design

Urban AI needs collective objectives.

URBAI’s results support a shift from purely user-optimal systems toward algorithms evaluated against emissions, spatial concentration, access, resilience, and fairness—under multiple adoption scenarios rather than a single benchmark.

→
The key design unit is not the isolated recommendation. It is the feedback loop between recommendations, human choices, and the urban system.
