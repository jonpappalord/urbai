Research

Understanding urban AI as a complex system.

URBAI connected individual mobility choices, algorithmic mediation, and city-wide externalities within a unified research programme.

The challenge

Urban platforms form socio-technical systems in which citizens and algorithms continuously influence one another. A route that is efficient for one driver may become inefficient—or harmful—when recommended to many. Similar feedback effects can emerge in ride-hailing, car sharing, venue recommendation, and residential choice.

Core question. How can AI assistants satisfy individual needs without degrading environmental quality, accessibility, safety, or social cohesion at the collective level?

1. Measure urban well-being

The project framed urban well-being as multidimensional. Rather than relying on travel time alone, it considered indicators related to environmental impact, road-network use, safety, accessibility, and spatial segregation.

  • Traffic and route concentration across the road network
  • Vehicle emissions and their spatial distribution
  • Accessibility to opportunities and urban services
  • Segregation, displacement, and neighbourhood transition

2. Model network effects

Controlled real-world experiments with proprietary platforms are often impossible. URBAI therefore used data-driven simulation and agent-based models to isolate mechanisms and compare counterfactual scenarios.

  • Mobility demand inferred from large-scale GPS traces
  • Road networks from OpenStreetMap
  • Microscopic traffic simulation with SUMO
  • Recommendations from multiple navigation providers
  • Schelling-inspired models of segregation and gentrification
  • Iterative human–AI feedback-loop simulations

3. Design next-generation assistants

The goal was not to reject algorithmic assistance, but to broaden its objective. URBAI explored how routing and recommendation could incorporate system-level signals and diversify choices while retaining usefulness for the individual.

Application domains

Navigation systems

Navigation services were studied as a concrete example of individual optimization producing network-scale consequences for congestion, emissions, and resilience.

Public mobility services

Ride-hailing and shared-mobility services motivated questions about accessibility, additional traffic, emissions, and unequal urban effects.

Residential dynamics

Agent-based models of relocation, segregation, and gentrification extended the project’s systems perspective to longer-term urban change.
